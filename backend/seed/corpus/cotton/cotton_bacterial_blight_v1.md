# Cotton Bacterial Blight (Xanthomonas citri pv. malvacearum)

## Symptoms
Angular, water-soaked spots on leaves, bounded by the leaf veins (giving
the spots a sharp-edged, angular shape rather than round); spots later
turn dark brown to black; on stems and the main axis, lesions run along
the length and are called "black arm" — can girdle and snap the stem in
severe cases; infected bolls develop black, water-soaked, sunken lesions
that can lead to boll rot and premature opening.

## Early Symptoms
Small, translucent, water-soaked angular spots on lower leaves, easy to
miss in early morning dew before they darken.

## Advanced Symptoms
Spots coalesce and blacken across the leaf; black-arm lesions girdling
stems; boll rot with visible black sunken patches; heavily affected
leaves drop early, thinning the canopy.

## Affected Parts
Leaves, stems (black arm), and bolls (boll rot) — all three, which is
unusual among the cotton diseases in this set and is itself a useful
field clue.

## Similar Diseases
Fusarium wilt also progresses to blackening and plant death in severe
cases, so a photo of an already-dying plant late in either disease can
look similar. The discriminator is the angular, water-soaked leaf spot,
present in bacterial blight and absent in fusarium wilt, which instead
shows vascular browning and wilting without discrete leaf lesions.
Pink bollworm also leaves damaged, discoloured bolls, but boll rot from
bacterial blight shows black, water-soaked surface lesions rather than
an internal larva.

## Causes
A seed-borne and rain-splash-spread bacterium. Infection is favoured by
warm, humid weather with frequent rain or heavy dew, and spreads within
a field through wind-driven rain and contact between wet leaves.

## Prevention
Use certified, disease-free seed — this disease is commonly carried on
seed coat, so seed source is the single biggest prevention lever. Avoid
working in or irrigating the field while foliage is wet, since bacteria
spread readily through water contact between plants. Crop rotation with
a non-host crop for at least one season reduces soil-carried inoculum.

## Cultural Management
Remove and destroy infected plant debris after harvest rather than
leaving it in the field, since the bacterium survives on crop residue.
Avoid overhead irrigation, particularly during flowering and boll
development, when boll infection risk is highest. Maintain wider plant
spacing where feasible to improve airflow and reduce the humid
microclimate the pathogen favours.

## Biological Management
Seed treatment with Pseudomonas fluorescens (talc-based formulation,
10 g/kg seed) before sowing suppresses seed-borne inoculum. Foliar
application of Pseudomonas fluorescens or Bacillus subtilis-based
biocontrol products during early vegetative stage can reduce disease
pressure where chemical use is being minimised.

## Chemical Management
Copper oxychloride 50% WP at 2.5 g/L water, PHI 7 days, re-entry 24
hours, applied at first sign of angular leaf spots and repeated at
10–14 day intervals if humid weather continues. Streptomycin sulphate
+ tetracycline combination (as locally registered) can be used as a
seed treatment before sowing in fields with a history of severe
outbreaks — this is a preventive seed-stage use, not a foliar rescue
spray once black arm has set in on mature plants.

## Monitoring
Inspect lower and mid-canopy leaves weekly from early vegetative stage,
especially after rain — early lesions are easiest to catch on lower
leaves before they climb the plant. Check stems for early black-arm
streaking on the main axis, not just leaves. During boll development,
examine bolls directly for early water-soaked surface spotting rather
than waiting for visible rot.

## When To Contact Expert
Black-arm girdling causing stem breakage on more than a few plants;
boll rot spreading past early-detected spots into widespread rot;
any outbreak following use of home-saved, uncertified seed, since
seed-source tracing matters for preventing recurrence next season.
