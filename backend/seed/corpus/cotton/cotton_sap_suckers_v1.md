# Cotton Sap Suckers — Whitefly & Thrips

## Symptoms
Whitefly: yellowing and downward curling of leaves, especially on the
upper canopy; sticky honeydew coating on leaves and top of bolls; black
sooty mould growing on the honeydew, giving leaves a dirty grey-black
sheen; small white insects fly up in a cloud when the plant is disturbed.

Thrips: silvery, streaky scarring concentrated on young leaves near the
growing tip; leaf margins curl upward and leaves look distorted or
crinkled rather than uniformly yellow; stunted terminal growth in heavy
infestations; no honeydew or sooty mould.

## Affected Parts
Undersides of leaves (both, where insects feed and rest) and the growing
tip (thrips especially — young, unexpanded leaves take the worst scarring).

## Similar Pests
Both cause yellowing, curling and distorted young leaves, so a single
top-down photo of curled leaves does not separate them. The reliable
field checks: tap the leaf over a white card or your palm — whitefly
adults fly up visibly; thrips are too small to see this way. Sticky
honeydew and black sooty mould are whitefly signatures and are never
produced by thrips.

## Causes
Whitefly (Bemisia tabaci) and thrips are both piercing-sucking insects
that remove plant sap and, in whitefly's case, also transmit cotton
leaf curl virus — so a whitefly infestation is a pest problem and a
disease-vector risk at the same time.

## Prevention
Avoid excess nitrogen, which produces the soft, sap-rich growth both
pests prefer. Maintain field-border sanitation — remove weed hosts like
Parthenium and Abutilon that carry whitefly populations between seasons.
Intercrop or border-crop with a trap crop (e.g. marigold) where locally
practised, to draw thrips away from the main crop during early growth.

## Cultural Management
Yellow sticky traps at 8–10/acre from 3 weeks after sowing intercept
whitefly adults before populations build. Blue sticky traps are more
effective specifically for thrips monitoring and mass trapping. Avoid
water stress, which increases plant susceptibility to both. Remove and
destroy heavily sooty-mould-coated lower leaves to reduce whitefly
breeding sites.

## Biological Management
Conserve and encourage natural enemies — Encarsia and Eretmocerus
parasitic wasps parasitise whitefly nymphs, and predatory mites and
anthocorid bugs feed on thrips. Avoid broad-spectrum insecticide sprays
early in the season purely on suspicion, since these kill the natural
enemy population and can trigger a whitefly resurgence worse than the
original infestation. Neem-based formulations (azadirachtin 1500 ppm at
2–3 ml/L) suppress both pests with lower impact on natural enemies.

## Chemical Management
Whitefly — Diafenthiuron 50% WP at 1 g/L water, PHI 7 days, re-entry
12 hours, applied when adult counts cross 8–10 per leaf on the upper
canopy. Thrips — Fipronil 5% SC at 2 ml/L water, PHI 15 days, re-entry
24 hours, at first sign of terminal scarring. Never apply a chemical
spray at bloom in a way that coincides with pollinator activity; spray
in the early morning or evening. Rotate insecticide groups between
consecutive sprays — repeated whitefly sprays with the same active
ingredient are a documented resistance risk in Maharashtra cotton belts.

## Monitoring
Count adult whitefly on 3 leaves (top, middle, lower canopy) per plant,
10 plants/acre, weekly from squaring onward. For thrips, examine 5
terminal leaves per plant for scarring and curling under a hand lens
if available. Track honeydew/sooty mould spread separately from raw
whitefly counts — mould coverage is a lagging indicator and by itself
underestimates an active infestation.

## When To Contact Expert
Whitefly counts persistently above threshold despite two rounds of
biological or chemical intervention; any leaf curling appearing alongside
vein thickening or small leaf-like outgrowths on the veins (possible
progression to cotton leaf curl virus, which needs a different
management path entirely); thrips damage on more than 25% of terminal
leaves at the seedling stage, where stunting can be yield-limiting.
