# Cotton Bollworm Complex — American Bollworm & Pink Bollworm

## Symptoms
American bollworm: round holes bored into squares, flowers and bolls; frass
(caterpillar droppings) visible at the entry hole; damaged seeds and stained
lint inside the boll; caterpillar itself is stout, colour varies from green
to brown with a pale stripe along the side.

Pink bollworm: flowers show a rosetted, fused-petal appearance before any
boll forms; entry holes on bolls are small and hard to spot from outside;
larvae inside are pink to rose-coloured, thinner than American bollworm
larvae; bolls fail to open fully even when mature.

## Affected Parts
Squares, flowers and bolls for both. Pink bollworm additionally damages
seed inside an already-formed boll, cutting lint quality even when the
boll looks intact from outside.

## Similar Pests
The two are frequently confused because both bore into bolls and leave a
damaged, discoloured boll behind. The single most reliable field check is
cutting the boll open: American bollworm larvae are green-brown, pink
bollworm larvae are unmistakably pink. Rosetted flowers, when present, are
diagnostic for pink bollworm and are not produced by American bollworm.

## Causes
Both are moth larvae (Lepidoptera). Adults lay eggs on squares, flower
buds and young bolls; larvae bore in immediately after hatching, so damage
is internal from the first instar onward, which is why external spotting
is hard until frass or a rosetted flower gives it away.

## Prevention
Grow Bt cotton hybrids where locally recommended, but pair with a
mandatory non-Bt refuge (typically 20% of the plot) to slow resistance
build-up in pink bollworm populations — this is now a widely reported
resistance-management gap in Maharashtra cotton belts.
Avoid late sowing, which extends the window bolls are exposed to peak
moth activity. Destroy crop residue and unopened bolls after harvest
so pink bollworm cannot carry over in stubble to the next season.

## Cultural Management
Deep summer ploughing to expose overwintering pupae to heat and predators.
Timely sowing within the recommended window for the region. Hand-pick and
destroy visibly infested squares and rosetted flowers early in the season,
before larvae bore deeper and become inaccessible. Avoid excess nitrogen,
which promotes lush growth attractive to egg-laying moths.

## Biological Management
Release Trichogramma chilonis egg parasitoid at first pest sighting,
repeated at 7–10 day intervals through peak flowering. Spray HaNPV
(Helicoverpa armigera nuclear polyhedrosis virus) at dusk for American
bollworm when larvae are still young (first–second instar), since NPV
needs to be ingested and is far less effective once larvae are boring
deep inside bolls. Pheromone traps (Helilure for American bollworm,
Gossyplure for pink bollworm) at 5 traps/acre for monitoring, or mass
trapping at higher density, disrupt mating before eggs are laid.

## Chemical Management
American bollworm — Emamectin benzoate 5% SG at 0.4 g/L water, PHI 5 days,
re-entry 12 hours; use at ETL (1 larva or 1 damaged square per plant).
Pink bollworm — Profenofos 50% EC at 2 ml/L water, PHI 15 days, re-entry
24 hours, once rosetted flowers or entry holes cross ETL. Chemical
control against pink bollworm is markedly less effective once larvae are
already inside a closed boll — this is why the cultural and biological
tiers above matter more for pink bollworm than for most cotton pests.
Rotate chemical classes between sprays rather than repeating the same
mode of action, to slow resistance.

## Monitoring
Install pheromone traps at flowering onset. Check traps every 2–3 days;
crossing 8–10 moths/trap/night for 2 consecutive nights signals a spray
window is approaching. Inspect 20 plants/acre weekly for squares/bolls
with entry holes or frass, and cut open 2–3 suspect bolls to confirm
larval colour before deciding a chemical spray is warranted.

## When To Contact Expert
Infestation crossing 10% damaged bolls despite biological measures;
any suspected pink bollworm resurgence in a Bt field (a known regional
resistance signal worth reporting); uncertainty distinguishing the two
species from a photo alone, since the management calendar genuinely
differs between them.
