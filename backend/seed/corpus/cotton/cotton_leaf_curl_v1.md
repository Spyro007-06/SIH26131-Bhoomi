# Cotton Leaf Curl Virus (CLCuV)

## Symptoms
Upward or downward curling of leaves; noticeably thickened veins on the
underside; small, cup-shaped or malformed leaf-like outgrowths (enations)
projecting from the vein undersides — this is the single most diagnostic
sign and is not produced by any insect feeding damage alone; general
stunting of the plant; boll formation and lint quality reduced in severe
cases.

## Early Symptoms
Mild upward curling on a few upper leaves; slight vein thickening,
easy to miss without turning the leaf over.

## Advanced Symptoms
Pronounced enations on most leaves; whole-plant stunting; internode
shortening giving the plant a bushy, compact look; sharply reduced
boll set.

## Affected Parts
Leaves primarily (curling, vein thickening, enations); indirectly the
whole plant's growth habit and boll load in advanced infection.

## Similar Diseases
Leaf curling and distortion also occur from whitefly feeding damage and
from thrips scarring, so curling on its own does not confirm CLCuV.
Enations along the vein underside are the discriminating sign — neither
whitefly nor thrips feeding produces them. Because whitefly is the
transmitting vector, an active whitefly infestation and an established
CLCuV infection can be present on the same plant at the same time.

## Causes
A begomovirus transmitted exclusively by whitefly (Bemisia tabaci) in a
persistent, circulative manner — the whitefly must feed on an infected
plant and then feed again to transmit, so vector control is the primary
lever against spread, not a direct cure once a plant is infected.

## Prevention
Plant CLCuV-tolerant/resistant hybrids where available for the region —
this is the single most effective preventive measure, since there is no
curative treatment once a plant is infected. Avoid planting near an
already-infected field or old cotton stubble carrying whitefly. Stagger
sowing away from known regional whitefly build-up windows where local
advisories flag them.

## Cultural Management
Rogue out (uproot and destroy) infected plants detected early, before
they become a whitefly-accessible virus source for the rest of the
field. Maintain field-border sanitation against whitefly-hosting weeds.
Avoid excess nitrogen, which produces the soft growth whitefly prefers
to colonise.

## Biological Management
There is no biological treatment for the virus itself. Biological
management here means suppressing the whitefly vector population —
conserve Encarsia/Eretmocerus parasitic wasps and use neem-based
sprays (azadirachtin 1500 ppm, 2–3 ml/L) rather than broad-spectrum
insecticides that kill natural enemies and can worsen whitefly pressure.

## Chemical Management
No chemical treatment cures CLCuV once a plant is infected — chemical
intervention here targets the whitefly vector only, to reduce further
spread within the field. Diafenthiuron 50% WP at 1 g/L water, PHI 7
days, re-entry 12 hours, applied when whitefly adult counts on infected-
field borders cross threshold. Spraying an already-infected plant does
not reverse its symptoms or restore yield on that plant.

## Monitoring
Walk the field weekly from early vegetative stage, scanning for upward
curling and vein thickening on upper leaves. Turn suspect leaves over
to check for enations before flagging a case — curling alone is not
sufficient. Track the count of newly infected plants week over week;
a rising count signals active whitefly transmission in progress, not
just historical infection.

## When To Contact Expert
Any new CLCuV detection in a field planted with a hybrid marketed as
tolerant — a resistance-breaking strain is a regional surveillance
priority. Infection spreading past early rogueing-out capacity (more
than roughly 5–10% of plants). Confirmation needed to distinguish CLCuV
from whitefly-only damage when photos are ambiguous.
