# Soybean Yellow Mosaic Virus (YMV)

## Symptoms
Yellow-green mosaic patches scattered irregularly across leaves;
mottling and crinkling of leaf tissue giving the leaf a puckered,
uneven surface; general plant stunting; reduced number of flowers and
pods, with any pods that do form often smaller and fewer-seeded than
on a healthy plant.

## Early Symptoms
Faint yellow speckling or mild mottling on younger trifoliate leaves,
easy to mistake for nutrient deficiency before the mosaic pattern and
crinkling become pronounced.

## Advanced Symptoms
Bright yellow-green mosaic covering most of the leaf surface;
noticeable leaf crinkling and puckering; visible plant stunting;
sharply reduced flowering and pod set.

## Affected Parts
Leaves primarily (mosaic mottling, crinkling); indirectly the whole
plant's growth habit, flowering and pod load in advanced infection.

## Similar Diseases
Nutrient deficiency (particularly iron or manganese) can cause a
similar yellowing pattern on soybean leaves, but true nutrient
deficiency yellowing is typically more uniform between the veins
without the mosaic mottling or leaf crinkling YMV produces. Crinkled,
puckered leaf texture alongside the mosaic pattern is the more
reliable YMV-specific sign.

## Causes
A begomovirus transmitted by whitefly (Bemisia tabaci) in a
persistent, circulative manner — as with cotton leaf curl virus, the
whitefly must feed on an infected plant and then feed again to
transmit, so vector control is the primary lever against spread, not
a cure once a plant is infected.

## Prevention
Plant YMV-tolerant/resistant varieties where available for the region
— there is no curative treatment once a plant is infected, so variety
choice is the single most effective preventive measure. Avoid sowing
adjacent to an already-infected field or old soybean stubble carrying
whitefly populations. Stagger sowing away from known regional whitefly
build-up windows where local advisories flag them.

## Cultural Management
Rogue out (uproot and destroy) infected plants detected early, before
they become a whitefly-accessible virus source for the rest of the
field. Maintain field-border sanitation against whitefly-hosting
weeds. Avoid excess nitrogen, which produces the soft growth whitefly
prefers to colonise.

## Biological Management
There is no biological treatment for the virus itself. Biological
management here means suppressing the whitefly vector — conserve
Encarsia/Eretmocerus parasitic wasps and use neem-based sprays
(azadirachtin 1500 ppm, 2–3 ml/L) rather than broad-spectrum
insecticides that kill natural enemies and can worsen whitefly
pressure.

## Chemical Management
No chemical treatment cures YMV once a plant is infected — chemical
intervention here targets the whitefly vector only, to reduce further
spread within the field. Diafenthiuron 50% WP at 1 g/L water, PHI 7
days, re-entry 12 hours, applied when whitefly adult counts on
infected-field borders cross threshold. Spraying an already-infected
plant does not reverse its symptoms or restore lost pod set.

## Monitoring
Walk the field weekly from early vegetative stage, scanning for
mosaic yellowing and leaf crinkling on younger trifoliate leaves.
Track the count of newly infected plants week over week; a rising
count signals active whitefly transmission in progress, not just
historical infection.

## When To Contact Expert
Infection spreading past early rogueing-out capacity (more than
roughly 5–10% of plants); any YMV detection in a field planted with a
variety marketed as tolerant, since a resistance-breaking strain is a
regional surveillance priority worth reporting.
