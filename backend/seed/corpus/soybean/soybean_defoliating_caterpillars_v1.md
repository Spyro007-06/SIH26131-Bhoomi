# Soybean Defoliating Caterpillars

## Symptoms
Irregular holes and missing leaf tissue scattered across the canopy;
in heavy infestations, feeding progresses to skeletonised leaves
(only the veins remain) and can strip a plant almost bare in a short
period once larvae reach later instars, which eat far more than
earlier ones.

## Affected Parts
Leaves across the canopy, worst on the upper and middle leaves where
larvae feed most actively; heavy defoliation indirectly reduces pod
fill by cutting the leaf area available for photosynthesis during pod
development.

## Similar Pests
Several different caterpillar species (tobacco caterpillar, green
semilooper, bihar hairy caterpillar, among others) produce similar
irregular-hole and skeletonising damage on soybean, and are grouped
here as one damage-pattern complex rather than separated, since the
management ladder below is materially the same across them. If a
specific larva is visible and identifiable in a photo, note its colour
and hair/spine pattern for the record even though initial management
does not usually depend on species-level identification.

## Causes
Moth larvae (Lepidoptera, multiple species) feeding on leaf tissue.
Populations build fastest in warm, humid conditions during vegetative
and early reproductive stages, and outbreaks are often patchy within a
field rather than uniform, since egg masses are laid in clusters.

## Prevention
Avoid dense, continuous soybean cropping across adjoining fields where
possible, since large uninterrupted host area lets populations build
faster. Maintain field-border sanitation to reduce weed hosts some of
these species also breed on between seasons.

## Cultural Management
Hand-pick and destroy visible egg masses and young larval clusters
early, before larvae disperse across the plant — this is most
effective in the first 1–2 instars, while larvae are still
concentrated near the egg mass. Avoid excess nitrogen, which produces
lush, more attractive foliage.

## Biological Management
Release Trichogramma chilonis egg parasitoid at first sighting of egg
masses, repeated at 7–10 day intervals. Spray Bacillus thuringiensis
(Bt) formulations targeting young larvae (first–third instar), since
Bt must be ingested and works far better before larvae reach the
later, more damage-heavy instars. Conserve natural predators (spiders,
predatory bugs) by avoiding unnecessary precautionary sprays.

## Chemical Management
Chlorantraniliprole 18.5% SC at 0.3 ml/L water, PHI 21 days, re-entry
24 hours, once defoliation crosses roughly 25–30% leaf area loss or
ETL for the specific field history. Apply in the evening to protect
pollinators and natural enemies active during the day. Rotate chemical
groups between sprays within a season to slow resistance build-up.

## Monitoring
Walk the field weekly from early vegetative stage, scanning for
irregular holes, skeletonised patches and visible larvae or egg
masses on the underside of leaves. Estimate defoliation percentage
across several representative plants rather than judging from the
worst-hit patch alone, since damage is often uneven within a field.

## When To Contact Expert
Defoliation crossing roughly 30–40% of total leaf area, especially
during pod-fill stage when the yield impact of lost leaf area is
highest; a patchy outbreak that keeps recurring in the same part of
the field across seasons, which can indicate an established local
breeding source worth investigating further.
