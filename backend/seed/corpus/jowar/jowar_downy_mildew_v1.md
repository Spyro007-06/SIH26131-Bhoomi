# Jowar Downy Mildew

## Symptoms
Chlorotic (pale yellow to white) streaks running lengthwise along the
leaf, following the leaf veins; a white to greyish downy fungal growth
visible on the underside of affected leaves, especially in humid
morning conditions — this fuzzy growth is the most diagnostic sign and
is not produced by any of jowar's other listed diseases; in systemic
infections the panicle itself can become malformed, showing leafy or
distorted structures instead of normal grain-bearing spikelets, and
affected plants are typically stunted.

## Early Symptoms
Faint pale-green to yellow streaking on younger leaves, most visible
early in the morning before the downy growth becomes obvious; easy to
miss without checking the leaf underside specifically.

## Advanced Symptoms
Pronounced white-grey downy growth coating much of the leaf underside;
extensive chlorotic streaking across most leaves; stunted plant
growth; malformed, leafy panicle structures replacing normal grain
formation in systemically infected plants.

## Affected Parts
Leaves (streaking, downy growth) and, in systemic infection, the
panicle itself, which can be malformed rather than producing normal
grain.

## Similar Diseases
Anthracnose also produces discoloured streaking or lesions on leaves,
but anthracnose's lesions are reddish-brown with dark dot-like fruiting
bodies, not pale chlorotic streaks with fuzzy white-grey downy growth
on the underside. Checking the leaf underside for the fuzzy downy
texture is the fastest way to separate the two in the field.

## Causes
An oomycete pathogen (Peronosclerospora sorghi), favoured by cool,
wet, humid conditions especially early in the season, and capable of
systemic infection — meaning it can spread from an infected seedling
into the whole plant, which is why advanced cases show panicle
malformation rather than just leaf symptoms. Soil-borne oospores allow
the disease to carry over between seasons independent of seed.

## Prevention
Use downy-mildew-tolerant varieties where available for the region.
Avoid sowing in fields with a known history of severe downy mildew
without a rotation break, since the pathogen persists in soil. Ensure
good field drainage, since cool, waterlogged conditions favour early
infection.

## Cultural Management
Rogue out (uproot and destroy) systemically infected plants showing
malformed panicles as early as identified, since these plants act as
an ongoing spore source for the rest of the field and cannot recover
to produce normal grain regardless of later treatment. Long crop
rotation (2–3 years) with a non-host crop reduces soil oospore levels
over time.

## Biological Management
Seed treatment with Trichoderma viride (4 g/kg seed) before sowing
gives young seedlings better resistance during the early infection
window when systemic establishment is most likely. There is limited
biological rescue once systemic infection and panicle malformation
have already occurred — prevention at the seedling stage matters far
more than any later intervention.

## Chemical Management
Seed treatment with Metalaxyl 35% WS at 6 g/kg seed before sowing is
the primary chemical lever, since it protects the plant during the
early systemic-infection window — foliar sprays on an already
systemically infected, malformed-panicle plant cannot reverse the
infection or restore normal grain formation. In fields with a strong
downy mildew history, a follow-up foliar spray of Metalaxyl + Mancozeb
combination at 2.5 g/L water, PHI 15 days, re-entry 24 hours, can be
applied at early vegetative stage as an additional protective layer.

## Monitoring
Check leaf undersides specifically, not just the top surface, from
early vegetative stage through a humid morning check — the downy
growth is most visible then and fades as leaves dry through the day.
As panicles emerge, inspect for malformed, leafy structures in place
of normal grain-bearing spikelets, which confirms systemic infection
in that plant.

## When To Contact Expert
Any systemic infection with panicle malformation appearing in a field
planted with a variety marketed as tolerant, since a resistance
concern is worth flagging regionally; downy mildew recurring across
seasons in the same field despite rotation, which can indicate a
persistently high soil oospore load needing a longer break or
different management approach.
